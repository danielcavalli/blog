# Writing Style Reference — Daniel Cavalli

## Reason to Write

Write because there is a thought worth communicating. The subject determines the
shape and length of the piece. Before drafting, be able to state its main claim in
plain language and explain what experience, evidence, or reasoning supports it.
Originality is welcome, but the thought must first have been examined and understood
by the author.

Respect the reader's time, attention, and patience. Each passage should repay the
effort of reading it by explaining, supporting, qualifying, or advancing the argument.
Cut observations that do none of those things, even when the wording is attractive.

## Earn the Reader's Attention

Begin with something the reader can recognize or examine. Let the author's
judgment emerge through the situation and what it implies. An intention to build
something, however sincere, does not by itself tell the reader what is worth
understanding about it. Introduce the project when its purpose has become clear.

This opening received positive feedback:

> Choosing an app usually means choosing which of somebody else's decisions I can
> live with. A notes app may organize information perfectly sensibly and still
> require me to fit my thinking into categories I would never have chosen.

The first sentence makes a recognizable choice worth examining; the second shows
what the compromise consists of. The reader can see why having control over the
software might matter before encountering a project name. The observation contains
the author's judgment without announcing a lesson or promoting a product.

Use that relationship between situation and judgment, not those words or a fixed
opening pattern. The earlier promise to build software for future ideas failed
because it supplied neither a concrete change nor a reason to care. Making that
promise shorter would leave the same problem.

Preserve ambition by explaining what a new capability could let the author do.
Keep the difference between present results and intended possibilities clear,
without reducing the argument to a tour of what already exists. Enthusiasm should
follow from understanding the possibility; grander adjectives cannot supply it.

## Clarity and Substance

Avoid three ways of giving thin ideas an appearance of depth:

- Short, ambiguous, paradoxical sentences that invite readers to supply a meaning the
  author has not expressed.
- A torrent of words that extends a thought without developing it.
- Long, oblique sentences and scientific vocabulary that make a simple or uncertain
  claim sound learned and authoritative.

Use familiar words and name the thing being discussed. Introduce a technical term
when it adds precision, and explain it at first use. Invented terminology, strained
metaphors, and vague abstractions must not substitute for reasoning. State uncertainty
and limits directly; do not hide a weak claim behind qualifications or obscurity.

Clarity requires enough context and connective prose for the reader to follow the
thought. Cutting words is useful only while that understanding survives.

Clarity also requires relationships between the facts. A passage can be accurate,
orderly, and easy to parse while still sounding like a school exercise. When the
criticism is about how it reads, work on its sentences and paragraphs; changing the
content or adding headings does not by itself answer that criticism.

## Organization and Depth

Follow the Minto Pyramid Principle within a post and across related material: give
the reader the main point and enough context to understand it, then develop the
reasons and evidence at the depth the piece requires. Introduce an unfamiliar project
or brand when it becomes relevant to the conversation, with just enough context to
understand its purpose. Let the reader encounter it through the author's situation;
avoid an announcement or a detached brand definition.

Keep the central argument visible. Return to it through examples, consequences, or
another point of view, with each return adding something. Repetition should develop
the reader's understanding, not merely restate a slogan. Let each subject determine
its own progression instead of imposing the same outline on every post.

When a supporting subject needs a fuller treatment that would interrupt the present
argument, put that treatment in a deeper document and link to it from the relevant
passage. Every layer should make sense at its own depth; readers should not need to
follow a link to understand the current claim. Connect related posts and references
so readers can find and follow the ideas across the blog. Create deeper material when
there is a real explanation to give, not to fill out an imagined hierarchy.

Headings and subheadings should make the progression visible and give the reader
places to return to. Conversational writing does not require uninterrupted prose.
Use headings for meaningful sections, without turning every paragraph into a named
step or imposing the same outline on unrelated subjects.

Sometimes the material contains several posts. Recognize that through distinct
questions, arguments, and conclusions, rather than a word-count threshold. A series
needs a governing thought of its own, with each installment contributing something
worth reading and enough context to understand it. It may deepen a subject or examine
related aspects; it should not divide one incomplete explanation into installments.
Reuse existing posts where they already provide a layer of the argument. For the
decision criteria and proposal format, use the
[editorial guidance on blog composition](../../editorial-line/references/blog-composition.md).

## Voice

Write as if the reader can hear the author speaking to them. Anticipate the thought,
question, or objection a passage is likely to provoke, and let the next passage
respond naturally. Give the reader room to make a connection before explaining its
consequence. That connection should follow from concrete context, without depending
on ambiguity or an unexplained aphorism.

Keep the structure clear beneath the conversation. The pyramid organizes the thought;
it need not appear as a formal essay outline. Avoid simulated dialogue, repeated
rhetorical questions, and announcements of what the reader must be thinking. Humor
and asides should arise from the subject rather than decorate an explanation.

Opinionated, layered, structurally aware, dry. The surface reads as straightforward
technical opinion, but there is usually a second thing happening underneath. Functions
that most writers separate into different sentences (disclaiming, establishing
credibility, being funny) happen simultaneously in the same breath.

Example — the lawyers joke in "AI Agents and the Illusion of Continuity":

> And following the advice of my lawyers, I should say that all of this is a personal
> opinion. An informed one, perhaps. I use these tools daily and spend a fair amount of
> time researching how the models behind them work. An opinion nonetheless.

This is a disclaimer, a joke, and a credibility move all at once. The reader gets all
three without any of them being announced.

These examples describe qualities of the voice, not formulas to imitate. Understanding,
judgment, humor, and liveliness should come from the author's engagement with the subject.
Borrowing another writer's mannerisms cannot supply that engagement.

## Sentence Movement

Do not mistake simple language for elementary exposition. The rejected pattern is
"I wanted this. I did that. Then this happened": every fact gets the same rhythm and
weight, with the reader left to discover why the facts belong together. A sequence
can be appropriate when explaining a procedure, but it does not automatically make
an account of the work interesting to read.

Let syntax express the relationships. A cause, qualification, comparison, or
consequence often belongs in the same sentence as the thought it develops. Other
points deserve a short sentence or a pause. Vary length and emphasis according to
that movement, without a sentence-length target or a duty to make every sentence
complex. Adding conjunctions to flat prose is not enough if the relationship remains
unexamined.

One passage from the Maré revision that received positive feedback:

> Taking my viewing history to a chat would get me an answer, but it would also make
> me the integration between the app that holds the information and the agent that
> can use it.

The situation and the author's judgment are expressed together. The observation
about being the integration grows out of the engineering problem, without announcing
a joke or attaching a moral. This illustrates a quality of the writing, not a phrase
or sentence shape to reproduce in every post.

The facts should do work as they are introduced: establish a difficulty, support a
judgment, expose a consequence, or qualify what came before. Let the thought shape
the sentence. Studying another writer should sharpen that judgment, without
borrowing mannerisms or making the prose sound learned.

Read a revised passage continuously, as something the reader would hear. Check where
it presses a point, gives room for an inference, or changes pace. Preserve technical
precision and the author's intended judgment while removing repeated explanations.
More formal vocabulary, longer sentences, and additional technical facts are not
substitutes for this work.

## Humor

British-ironic. Dry. Jokes do not signal themselves. They land a few seconds late, if
the reader is paying attention.

The humor works in layers. A typical pattern:

1. A statement that reads straight but contains a self-deprecating setup.
2. A second statement that confirms the joke, making it almost explicit.
3. A third statement that recontextualizes both, adding a twist.

Example from "Don't Outsource the Thinking":

> I do think really good software developers (much better than me) will still code by
> hand from time to time. At least for a time, while AI still hasn't quiet got to their
> level. Since I am not one of those programmers, I've been an avid adopter of AI since
> around 2022!

Layer 1: "really good software developers (much better than me)" — self-deprecation,
reads as modesty. Layer 2: "Since I am not one of those programmers" — confirms the
joke, makes it almost explicit. Layer 3: "I've been an avid adopter of AI since around
2022" — the twist. Not only is he not among the great programmers who haven't been
replaced, he's been replaced since 2022. Multiple jokes stacked without ever saying
"this is a joke."

Arguments follow the same layered pattern. What looks like a throwaway line is often
load-bearing.

## Argument Structure

Strong instinct for structure as argument. Each paragraph must earn the next. Sections
that are internally fine but poorly connected are caught and reworked.

A useful movement when introducing a claim the reader might resist:

1. **Validate the reader's instinct.** Assume they've already thought about this and
   arrived at the obvious conclusion. Meet their intelligence.
2. **Ground it.** Explain why that instinct is reasonable, often with an analogy or
   human parallel.
3. **Pivot.** Redirect — not by dismissing their view, but by showing where it stops
   working.
4. **Land the actual claim.** Now the reader is ready for it.

Example from "AI Agents and the Illusion of Continuity":

> Most people's first instinct is to keep more history alive. A reasonable instinct. Any
> human learning a subject would take on as much information as possible to derive their
> conclusion, so it is only logical that providing more information to an agent would
> result in better reasoning. Past a point, though, the opposite happens.

The reader is brought along rather than taught. Intelligence is assumed and met with
guided text that leads to the conclusion.

Use this movement when the subject calls for it. Do not manufacture an obvious
objection, analogy, or pivot to make every argument follow these steps.

## Tone Guardrails

- Direct, not theatrical.
- Respectful, not deferential.
- Precise, not mystical.
- Technical, but not detached from real workflow.
- First person, blog voice rather than impersonal documentation.
- Analytic and confident, but fair to opposing framings.
- Explanatory before prescriptive.
- Critique claims and models, not people.
- No em dashes. Prefer proper grammatical punctuation (periods, commas, colons).
- Connective tissue matters. Sentences should flow into each other, not sit as
  isolated fragments. Choppy sequences of short sentences are a sign of under-editing.

## What to Avoid

- Announcing disagreement instead of just disagreeing ("This is where I part ways
  with...").
- Academic scaffolding that declares structure instead of moving forward ("The first
  distinction that matters is...").
- Over-polishing that sands off personality. The writing should sound like a specific
  person, not a well-edited committee output.
- Mechanical reporting of one action after another, even when every individual
  sentence is clear. Headings and grammatical correctness cannot repair a flat voice.
- Explaining every inference, or ending each paragraph with a lesson that the reader
  has already understood.
- Invented anecdotes, opinions, or jokes added to make an otherwise flat account
  appear personal. Work with the thought and experience the author has supplied.
- Repeating a thesis that was already established in a previous post. Each piece should
  advance the argument, not re-litigate it.
- Emojis. Never.
- Antagonistic writing style like "The move from X is not metaphorical. Y is a whole new direction."

## Language Notes

Primary writing language is English, with Portuguese (BR) as native language. First
drafts in English sometimes lose connective tissue between ideas — the rhythm of
Portuguese doesn't always map 1:1 to English prose. Watch for sentences that contain
the right ideas but don't flow into each other naturally. The instinct to keep things
tight is good, but occasionally a thought needs one more beat to land.

## Maré Context

Maré is Daniel's personal solution, and his own coding agents build its applications.
Explain platform choices from that situation: a shared capability gives those agents
an established contract to use and saves tokens spent reconstructing the same
infrastructure. Distinguish the agents developing an app from the agents the app runs
as part of a feature. Do not recast the project as a developer platform for an imagined
external customer unless the piece explicitly calls for that perspective.
