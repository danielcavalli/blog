# Prose Contract

## Purpose

Prose must communicate thought with precision and an appropriate voice. Reduce
unnecessary words and cognitive load without flattening meaning or rhythm. Apply
sentence-level rules only after the information hierarchy is correct. Do not
use polished prose to disguise a document with the wrong purpose, audience,
scope, or structure. Conversely, sound content and organization do not ensure
good prose. When the reader objects to how a piece is written, diagnose its
sentences and paragraphs before changing its subject or outline.

## Give abstractions something to explain

A sentence can be grammatically clear while communicating almost nothing.
Broad intentions such as making better tools or enabling future possibilities
need an intelligible object and consequence. Identify what changes for
someone using the software, making the decision, or doing the work. Give the
reader enough of that situation to understand the claim.

Let the relationship between the facts carry the author's judgment. A tool
can do its intended job well while imposing an unsuitable way of working;
explaining that relationship gives a claim about personal control something
to mean. Merely naming flexibility or a shared foundation leaves the reader
to supply the argument. Use the relationship warranted by the material, not
this example's sentence pattern.

When the author's own activity bears on the subject, it can serve as evidence
inside the argument. A choice made while doing the work can demonstrate the
principle being discussed. Do not invent an experience or force this device
into every piece.

## Use assertion-first compression

Reduce each paragraph to the distinct assertions its reader needs, then
identify how they relate: cause, condition, consequence, comparison,
qualification, or contrast. Reconstruct that thought in prose. The assertion
list is a working aid, not a requirement to give each fact its own sentence.

Keep qualifications that affect meaning, stance, scope, behavior, ownership,
or a required condition. Give the important thought emphasis and let supporting
material occupy its proper place, whether within the same sentence or across
several. Minimum sentence count is not the objective.

Common failures include:

- announcing a distinction instead of stating it;
- framing a responsibility as conditional acceptability;
- listing consequences already implied by the governing rule; and
- repeating one assertion as a claim, explanation, and conclusion.

Prefer direct allocation:

> Reviewers recommend changes. Authors approve changes.

over defensive qualification:

> Reviewer involvement is acceptable only when recommendations remain subject
> to approval by the author.

## Put the point first

State the main subject and verb early.

Prefer:

> The author owns the final wording.

over:

> The final wording, which must remain independent of reviewer preferences,
> is owned by the author.

Make the governing point easy to find, normally near the opening of the
paragraph. A reader scanning paragraph openings should follow the argument,
but this is a comprehension test, not a demand for identical topic sentences.
A brief orientation or a condition may need to come first for the point to land.

## Let syntax express relationships and emphasis

There is no preferred sentence length independent of the thought. Use a short
sentence when the point deserves emphasis or the reader needs a pause. Use a
longer one when a cause, reservation, comparison, or consequence belongs with
the statement it qualifies. Separate unrelated claims; keep related claims
together when doing so makes their relationship easier to grasp.

Avoid a repeated pattern of "I wanted X. I did Y. Then Z happened." The facts
may be correct while the prose gives them all equal weight and leaves their
significance undeveloped. Changing the sentence openings or joining every
pair with "because" will not by itself repair that problem.

For example, these are the same situation expressed with different emphasis:

> The app stores my history. I copy it into a chat. The chat returns
> recommendations. I copy them into the app.

> Taking my history to a chat would get me recommendations, but it would leave
> me moving information between the app that stores it and the agent that can
> use it.

The second version makes the author's involvement the point. It does not
become clearer by being longer; its clauses expose a relationship that the
first merely records as a sequence. Choose the relationship justified by the
source, rather than inventing a lesson for every paragraph.

Long sentences can fail just as badly when nested interruptions conceal the
main clause. Do not compensate for elementary prose with ornate vocabulary,
unnecessary subordination, strained metaphors, or manufactured profundity.
Procedures and incident timelines can properly use a sequence of actions;
judge whether that sequence serves the reader's actual purpose.

## Write one topic per paragraph

A paragraph develops a governing thought through related explanation:

```text
claim -> necessary explanation -> consequence
```

These are logical functions, not three compulsory sentences. An explanation
may already make the consequence clear; do not append a restatement to finish
the pattern. Start a new paragraph when the focus changes or the reader needs
a pause. Paragraph length follows the development of the thought, not a quota.

A common subject does not make several arguments one thought. Identify how
each assertion supports, qualifies, or develops the paragraph's point. Give
an independent consequence room elsewhere. Merely inserting paragraph breaks
does not supply the reasoning that must connect those passages.

## Give the reader room to participate

Supply the context a connection requires, then trust the reader to make it.
Leaving an implication unstated works only when the facts make it available.
Withholding the premise or the significance asks the reader to invent the
thought rather than participate in it.
Do not explain an implication twice, announce what the reader must be thinking,
or add a question-and-answer exchange merely to sound conversational. At the
same time, do not remove necessary transitions and expect a list of facts to
hold together by itself.

The author's judgment can appear in the selection, relationship, and emphasis
of facts. It need not be announced with "the lesson is" or supported by an
invented anecdote. An aside or a dry observation may carry meaning as well as
voice; do not delete it merely because it is not another technical assertion.

## Prefer direct statements

Use positive, declarative language when possible.

Prefer:

> The editor accepts revisions from the author.

over a negative construction that asks the reader to infer the permitted path.
Avoid double negatives.

Prefer active ownership statements:

> The editor manages the publication schedule.

over:

> The publication schedule is managed by the editor.

Passive voice remains appropriate when the actor genuinely does not matter.

## Remove linguistic indirection

Prefer one precise word over a phrase when both convey the same meaning:

- `use`, not `make use of`;
- `because`, not `due to the fact that`;
- `to`, not `in order to`.

Remove phrases that announce the prose instead of contributing information:

- "It is important to note that";
- "It is worth mentioning that";
- "As previously discussed";
- "In the context of";
- "This essentially means that".

State the information directly.

## Use simple language for complex ideas

Technical complexity does not require linguistic complexity. Prefer familiar
words when they preserve the technical meaning. Do not use elaborate vocabulary
to make a subject sound more sophisticated.

Avoid jargon when a precise ordinary term exists. Keep domain terminology when
it represents an actual domain distinction.

## Preserve important distinctions

Simple language must not flatten the subject. Do not replace precise terms
with broader words merely to make prose shorter.

For example, do not replace authorship, review, and approval with "oversight"
when their distinct responsibilities matter.

Do not replace a draft, a revision, and a published edition with "document"
when they represent different objects.

Clarity is the simplest language that preserves the required distinction.

## Avoid unnecessary modifiers

Prefer concrete nouns and verbs over chains of adjectives and noun modifiers.
Avoid more than two noun modifiers when possible.

Prefer:

> process for approving revisions

over:

> document revision approval management process

Use a short explanatory phrase, such as "the editor who manages revisions,"
when a compound noun would be harder to parse.

## Make references explicit

Replace ambiguous pronouns with the relevant noun.

Prefer:

> The author sends the draft to the editor. The editor verifies the draft's
> references.

over:

> The author sends it to the editor, who verifies them.

Repeat a noun when repetition improves comprehension. Do not optimize for
stylistic variety; consistent repetition often increases precision in technical
documentation.

## Establish conditions before consequences

When behavior depends on a condition, establish the condition before the
result.

Prefer:

> If the evidence is incomplete, the author qualifies the claim.

over:

> The author qualifies the claim if the evidence is incomplete.

This order gives readers the context before asking them to interpret the
consequence.

## Use modal verbs deliberately

Avoid vague modal language. Do not use `should` when the statement can express a
requirement, recommendation, current fact, proposal, future decision, or genuine
possibility more precisely.

Use:

- `must` for a requirement: "The author must verify the quotation."
- a direct present-tense statement for a current fact;
- an attributed statement for a proposal: "The proposed process includes a
  second review."
- `might` for a genuine possibility: "The wording might change after review."
- `may` for official permission or policy.

Use `would` and `could` for actual alternatives and counterfactuals. Preserve
the author's distinction between an intention, an opinion, and an implemented
fact. Do not flatten personal reasoning into specification language merely to
remove modal verbs.

## Keep lists conceptually parallel

Sibling items must represent comparable types of information and use parallel
grammatical structures.

Prefer:

> The editor reviews:
>
> - factual accuracy;
> - argument structure;
> - terminology consistency;
> - sentence clarity.

Avoid mixing responsibilities, actions, components, and systems in one list. If
items cannot be expressed in parallel, reconsider whether they belong in the
same group.

## Define terms once and use them consistently

Explain unfamiliar terms and abbreviations at first meaningful use, judging
familiarity against the intended audience. A technical reader does not need
every common acronym laboriously expanded. After establishing a domain term,
use it consistently; do not introduce synonyms merely for stylistic variety.

If "revision" is the established term, do not alternate among revision,
edition, draft, and release unless those terms intentionally represent
different concepts.

## Make links carry meaning

Link text must describe the concept or destination. Name the concept being
expanded rather than using "this document" or "here" as link text.

Links let the reader descend deliberately through the documentation hierarchy.

## Optimize for comprehension, not minimum word count

Some repetition is useful. Repeat a subject, noun, or qualification when
removing it creates ambiguity.

Do not combine:

> The author owns the argument. The author also owns the wording.

into:

> The author owns the argument and wording.

unless both properties truly share the same scope and qualifications.

The goal is economical wording that preserves comprehension and the intended
voice. A shorter version that becomes choppy, overexplicit, or anonymous has
not improved the prose.

## Follow the compression priority

When shortening text, apply changes in this order:

1. Remove duplicated information.
2. Move lower-level information deeper in the documentation hierarchy.
3. Replace groups of details with a precise abstraction.
4. Remove linguistic filler.
5. Simplify sentence structure.
6. Shorten individual phrases.

Check the information structure before compressing sentences. Once it is
sound, treat criticism of flat or mechanical writing as a prose problem;
do not keep restructuring the piece in lieu of writing it well.

## Apply the final sentence test

For every sentence, ask:

> What does this sentence contribute to the thought or its expression?

Information, inference, necessary orientation, emphasis, or an effect integral
to the voice can justify a sentence. Repeating an implication the reader has
already understood, or merely announcing the prose, usually cannot. Do not
use "voice" to defend filler that does none of this work.

Then ask:

> What changes if each qualification is removed?

Remove qualifications that do not affect meaning, stance, scope, behavior, or
a required condition. Finally, read the paragraph continuously: do the
sentences develop the thought, or could they be rearranged with little effect
because they merely enumerate facts? Preserve purposeful sequence where the
sequence is the subject. Evaluate rhythm and comprehension through reading,
not sentence-length targets or a checklist of transition words.
