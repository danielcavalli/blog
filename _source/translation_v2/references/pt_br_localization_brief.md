Target: publishable Brazilian Portuguese technical-editorial prose.

This is localization, not sentence transport. The translated text must read as if the same author had written it directly in Portuguese for Brazilian readers.

Method:
- Preserve meaning, voice, argument structure, caveats, and humor.
- Rebuild sentence movement, connective tissue, and clause order when literal transfer sounds imported from English.
- Prefer the wording a Brazilian technical writer would naturally publish, even when that requires full sentence rewrites.
- Preserve claims, agency, uncertainty, and emphasis through Brazilian syntax. Grammatical subjects and clause order may change; the author's stance and rhetorical devices must survive.

Brazilian voice in a personal article:
- Read the Portuguese on its own. It should sound like the author explaining something to an intelligent Brazilian reader, with technical confidence and ordinary, connected phrasing.
- Avoid drifting into impersonal institutional prose or a mixture of Brazilian and European usage. Do not manufacture informality with slang, filler, or dropped qualifications.
- Rebuild awkward abstractions as natural expressions of the same thought. "It can justify the effort by being useful to me" can become "Se o app for útil para mim, o trabalho de criá-lo pode valer a pena." The change of grammatical subject is legitimate; the possibility and personal measure of value remain.
- Do not require a literal "pode" for every English "can", or the same noun for every English metaphor. Check what the whole passage asserts before diagnosing a loss of meaning.
- Match the author's actual register, including irritation, enthusiasm, blunt judgments, jokes, and exaggeration. Do not turn a personal blog into a sober institutional report. "Serious" means taking the meaning seriously, not making the speaker more restrained.
- Do not dilute a categorical claim with "pode", "tende a", "em geral", or "busca" unless the source qualifies it. Do not turn a sarcastic assertion into a sincere explanation or explain the joke to the reader.
- Preserve a metaphor that works naturally in Brazil, especially when later paragraphs return to it. "Plumbing" can be "encanamento" in an extended infrastructure metaphor; replacing every occurrence with "infraestrutura de base" erases the author's concrete image.

Borrowings and foreignisms:
- Decide borrowings artifact-wide, not occurrence by occurrence.
- Keep English when Brazilian technical usage genuinely expects the English form.
- Localize when Brazilian editorial usage naturally prefers Portuguese.
- Keep product names, platform names, branded terms, and hard identifiers exactly as named.
- Avoid socially wrong half-decisions, such as translating a term in one paragraph and borrowing it in the next without a contextual reason.

Punctuation and rhythm:
- Localize sentence rhythm while preserving deliberate rhetorical punctuation. Parenthetical asides must remain in parentheses, around the same thought; translate their contents naturally. Never turn them into comma-delimited clauses, dashes, or separate sentences, or move their contents into the main assertion.
- Preserve deliberate exclamations, ellipses, and scare quotes when they carry enthusiasm, hesitation, or irony. Do not tidy away the author's personality.
- Use the travessão only when it genuinely improves the sentence; do not abuse it.
- Do not use a comma before the opening travessão in inciso structures.
- Recast overlong English sentences when Brazilian Portuguese would read better with different clause structure or cadence.
- Use semicolons sparingly in conversational blog prose. Do not copy them from English to preserve sentence shape; choose a period, a connective, or a recast sentence when it reads more naturally. Do not mechanically replace semicolons with commas.

Discourse and rhetoric:
- Preserve argumentative layering: a sentence may be stating a fact, softening a disagreement, making a joke, and framing credibility at the same time.
- Do not flatten connective texture into generic explanatory prose.
- Preserve restrained emphasis, contrast, caveats, and understated irony when present.

Register:
- Serious, readable, technically precise Brazilian prose.
- Not bureaucratic officialese.
- Not generic neutral “LLM Portuguese”.
- Not literal English with Portuguese words.

Common failure patterns to avoid:
- literal calques like "puxar fios" for "pull threads"
- clause ordering that feels copied from English
- punctuation that reflects English pacing instead of Portuguese syntax
- mixed English/Portuguese term handling within the same artifact
- prose that is understandable but does not sound like it belongs to the Brazilian language community

Review authority:
- A critique must show an actual loss or alteration of meaning, voice, or native fluency. A different grammatical subject or sentence structure is not itself a mistranslation.
- Reject a proposed correction when it introduces a calque or makes good Brazilian phrasing sound translated. Explain the decision using the source meaning and this locale policy.
